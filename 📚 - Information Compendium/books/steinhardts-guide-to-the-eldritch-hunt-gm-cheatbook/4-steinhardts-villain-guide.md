# Steinhardt's Villain Guide
*Source: Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: GM Cheatbook, p. 41* 

This section explains how to make remarkable villains who create unforgettable adventures for you and your players.

Complex antagonists with strong motivations react to players and propel the story forward. Well-crafted villains provide tension, meaningful choices, and a world that feels alive. Players feel invested when their actions directly impact a villain's cunning plans. Adaptable villains survive setbacks with new schemes, keeping stakes high. Memorable traits and dramatic introductions establish them as compelling threats. So let's dive in!

## A Campaign Driven by Villains and Players

Villains are not static entities sitting atop a throne waiting for the characters to show up; their goals and strategies continuously evolve. They're doing things. Their minions raid towers filled with ancient weapons, research eldritch spells in gothic towers, and establish underground lairs. The characters can see these actions and attempt to counter them. The villain evolves. They revise their plans. The story keeps going. Some villains die, and new ones rise.

## Three Villains

A campaign can feel flat with only one villain—just stop the Big Bad and you win. But add two or three villains and things get messy in a good way.

With a couple different bad guys running around causing trouble, their plans start crashing into each other. Maybe they team up to cause more chaos. Maybe they turn against each other. Or they each do their thing in their corner, forcing the party into trade-offs when they pick one to pursue. Regardless, it gives the players more to react to.

Having multiple types of villains each acting simultaneously also opens up more adventure possibilities; an evil priest presents different quests than a corrupt king. Mixing up villain types keeps things exciting for the players. And if the heroes beat one villain early, you've still got back-ups to drive the story. The world keeps on turning without that one bad apple.

But before creating three villains, let's see how the villain creation process goes for one.

It takes about 10 minutes to list out everything about a villain, but you only need to do this once, and it will last you for an entire villain arc of your campaign, saving you hours down the line!

## Villains

*Or how to create villains that feel smart and not forced.*

To create a villain, define its key traits and rank them in order of importance. This will help to shape the villain's goal and their actions toward it. You can also work backward, and instead choose a goal you want to see a villain work toward, and then create traits for a villain with that ambition.

To determine the villain's key traits, consider the following questions. You don't need an answer for each, but it's helpful to decide objectives and methods using 3 to 5 traits.

### Backstory & Motivation:

What past events shaped the villain's current worldview and goals?

What drives them to pursue their aims, whether it's revenge, ambition, love, or something else?

### Personality:

How does the villain behave in various situations?

What are their dominant personality traits? Are they cunning, brash, meticulous, or charismatic?

### Ethics & Morality:

Does the villain see themselves as evil, or do they believe they're doing the right thing?

What is their moral code, and where might they draw the line?

### Emotional Depth:

What are the villain's fears, hopes, loves, and regrets?

Providing emotional depth can make a villain more relatable and memorable.

### Appearance & Presence:

How does the villain present themselves to the world?

Consider their physical appearance, attire, and the aura or vibe they exude.

### Environment & Lair:

Where does the villain spend most of their time?

Consider the design and defenses of their base or lair, as well as its location.

### Symbolism & Themes:

What larger themes or symbols does the villain represent? Some examples include chaos, tyranny, nature's wrath, or the dangers of unchecked ambition.

- **Symbolism common in Victorian horror.** Decay and Ruin, Forbidden Knowledge or Secrets, Isolation, Duality of Human Nature, Madness, Cursed Existence, Romantic Tragedy, Nature's Revenge  
- **Symbolism found in Lovecraftian horror.** Humanity's Insignificance, Incomprehensible Entities, Isolation and Hopelessness, Inevitable Doom, Forbidden Knowledge, Legacy and Ancestry, Madness and Obsession, Inhumanity (The idea that humanity is not alone, and not even dominant, in the universe)  

### Methods & Modus Operandi:

How does the villain typically operate?

Do they prefer deception, brute force, magic, or manipulation?

### Goals

This aspect of your villain is crucial, and the reason it gets its own title. It is not an option, but a requirement.

What is the villain's objective? Ambitions beyond that?

Are they seeking world domination, the destruction of a rival, or maybe redemption?

### Example Villain: Malach, the Angel of Flesh

The first manikin given full sentience, he saw the atrocities his masters were committing towards his kin. Fueled with rage, he made his escape, swearing to free his kin from the clutches of those who bear flesh and rid the world of their existence.

#### _Key traits:_


- Hatred of Flesh  
- Love for Manikins  
- Cunning and Deceitful  

This will help shape the actions of [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) when you roleplay him or think of his course of actions.

#### _Goals:_


> [!quote]  
> 
> The Hour of Flesh is over. Now begins the Age of the Machines.

Destroy all beings of flesh in Luyarnha, and turn it into a city of manikins.

### Roleplaying the Villain

Once you've determined and ordered the traits and goals for a villain, you can more easily decide what choices they'll make and how their plans will unfold.

For example, [Malach's](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) hatred of flesh is stronger than his love for manikins, so in difficult situations, he will not hesitate to sacrifice his kin if it means a greater number of beings of flesh will die. But because he loves manikins, he is unlikely to exert a tyrannical rule over them, unless he becomes really desperate. Finally because he is cunning, he will have multiple plans to achieve his goal. This should be true of all villains, but this one should have additional backups for each step of his plans.

### Constraints

What are the limitations of the villain? If the villain was omnipotent, they might win immediately, and it would not make for a very fun campaign.

So, all villains have strengths, weaknesses, resources, and relationships. These can be from within the stat block itself or in context with the lore. Use the following questions to help outline the limits of a villain.

#### Strengths & Resources:

What skills, powers, or resources does the villain possess?

This could be magical abilities, vast wealth, political influence, or a keen intellect.

#### Flaws & Vulnerabilities:

What are the villain's blind spots or Achilles' heels?

This could be a physical weakness, an emotional tie, overconfidence, or a moral code they won't break.

#### Relationships & Allies:

Who are the villain's allies, minions, or friends? Enemies?

Consider the dynamics of these relationships. Are they based on fear, loyalty, mutual benefits, or love?

#### Example: Malach's Constraints

[Malach, the Flesh Angel](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) has the following constraints:

##### _Strengths:_


- Can talk to any construct, and given enough time, convert them to his cause  
- Unknown whereabouts  
- High Intellect  
- Immunity to diseases and poison  

##### _Flaws:_


- Frail (relative to other CR 15 creatures)  
- Few allies that he can or is willing to collaborate with  
- Limited resources (both information and materials)  

## Creating the Villain's Plan

Now for the exciting part: how the villain will take over the world—or at least commit their villainous plan.

The nefarious scheme could be as simple as robbing a bakery, but heroes need exceptional villains with challenging goals, otherwise it's not worthwhile to go after them. Why try to arrest Nix the jaywalker, when the party could be spending time trying to end the cult that wishes to summon Cthulhu and destroy the universe? Priorities people!

The villain now has traits, strengths, flaws, and a goal. Using these, construct a plan the villain will use to achieve their goal. Include four to six steps—a balance between complexity and opportunities for the heroes to foil the plan before catastrophic failure.

### Example: Malach's Plan

When making [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) the villain, I started with his primary goal—getting rid of flesh creatures and making a city of just manikins.

I knew [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) didn't have a lot of help or resources to start with, so I planned backwards from his end goal. First he'd need to learn how to make more manikins to build an army. Once he could make manikins, he'd need to sneakily get materials and set up workshops to start manufacturing the army without anyone noticing.

With a robot army ready, he'd need a way to take down most of the population all at once. So, he'd work on making a deadly poison and find a way to spread it widely. Finally, after the poison did its job, the manikin army could finish off whoever was left.

By looking at what the villain wants and what he's got to work with, I could map out the steps he'd need to accomplish his goal. Thinking it through logically like this can help GMs figure out plans that make sense for their bad guys.

In bullet points, the plan looks like this:

- Learn how to create manikins  
- Gather the materials necessary to create manikins and toxins  
- Create manikins and toxic bombs  
- Release the toxin in key locations  
- Unleash army to reap the remainder of the survivors  
- Bask in world free of creatures of flesh  

### Evolving and Resilient Plots

Of course, during the game, the characters likely thwart some of these steps. What happens then? What happens when the characters destroy the supply convoy with the necessary materials? What if the characters eliminate the electric supply of the hangars where the manikins are being created? What if they expose the infiltrated manikins before they can act?

Our villain's steps must evolve. The villain's plot needs to be resilient. One way to do so is to give each step a backup step. And if they're really smart, perhaps two backup steps. What can they do to keep their plan progressing even when their steps are being obstructed?

#### Example: Steps Required for Malach's Plan

- **Learn how to create manikins.**   
    Only the Scions know how to create manikins. He can try to kidnap a high ranking member, or he could sneak into their forbidden library pretending to be a manikin and steal the knowledge for himself. Seeing as there are multiple people with the know-how, if the first kidnapping doesn't work, the second or third should do the trick.  
- **Gather the materials necessary to create manikins and toxins.**   
    If the characters blow up Malach's convoy, he knows that the Scions have reserves. He can negotiate with them, using a fake name if necessary. Finally, if that plan gets exposed, he can always sneak in and steal what he needs—not ideal, but doable.  
- **Create manikins and toxic bombs.**   
    If the party manages to destroy all his manikins in the midst of their constructions, he will have to lay low for a while until he can rebuild. A big set-back of course, but one he can recover from as long as he isn't caught in the midst of a battle.  

    He needs substances that rot flesh but not metal. If he needs an alternate source of toxins, Carmella's Greenhouse is the only other source. If he doesn't manage to get his hands on it through purchasing, he can always raid the house with his manikins and frame the Scions. And if that assault gets repelled, he'll have no choice but to not rely on the toxins.  

    If purchasing or stealing the toxins doesn't succeed, he could attempt to make a larger army to defeat both the Church and the Scions. Alternatively, he knows that the Scourge is rampant in the Ruby District, and he would not hesitate to break the barriers that force the lockdown in order to spread the Scourge further in the city to weaken it.  
- **Release the toxin in key locations.**   
    In case the characters expose the infiltrated manikins, he has rigged each of them with poisonous explosives that rot flesh but not steel. It would kill fewer people than ideal, but that might be enough for the rest of his forces to sweep through the town.  
- **Unleash army to reap the remainder of the survivors.**   
    Ideally he'll sit back and enjoy the destruction from afar. But if his army has been weakened prior, he'll be forced to join the battle—a resort he is loath to take, as it leaves him vulnerable.  
- **Bask in world free of creatures of flesh.**   
    Now [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) can sit back and enjoy a nice break after a hard-earned purge. Or perhaps he'll set his sights on the rest of the world.

### Update the Villain's Plan

While preparing each session, think, "what is my villain up to?" This helps you understand how the story changes based on what the characters are doing. Remember, villains have their plans and steps to reach their goals. Think of these steps as a timer, showing the players the villain's progress.

Villains drive the story of our games. Exciting villains create tension for the players, have compelling motivations, and adapt their strategies to the actions of the characters.

### Stay Adaptable

The story won't progress as you expect it to. Players will foil the villain's plans, and the villain will need to adapt. Instead of planning for all possible situations, it's better to be ready to change things in the moment.

To do so, immerse yourself in your villains' plan—internalize their backgrounds, motivations, behaviors, and probable actions. This will help you adjust when players do the unexpected. When you plan your next session, focus on what the villains are currently doing. It will make the world feel more alive.

### Failure is an Option

Maybe [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) just loses. The backup plan fails. The heroes are successful. You have an entire cast of villains you can create. When one villain gets thwarted, two others move their plans forward.

Now that [Malach](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/npc/malach-angel-of-flesh-sgtteh.md) is out the way, the more nefarious elements of the Scions will have free reign across both the Newbridge and Shrouded districts, and the drug dealers that were held in their place by the terrifying manikins would be free to expand their trade more aggressively.

When a villain is cornered, they might make one last desperate plunge. Maybe they have an all-out attack against the characters. Maybe they conduct a last desperate move. Or maybe they flee or are arrested.

### Don't Force the Plot

A compelling TTRPG's plot creates an immersive experience for the players. The antagonist directly challenges the characters' values in complex ways. NPCs have close ties to the conflict, raising the stakes. Tension builds through escalating threats, with occasional triumphant moments. Mysterious clues foreshadow big reveals. Surprising yet logical twists keep players engaged. Impactful themes encourage discussion beyond the game. The GM paces the story well, tying up loose ends with a satisfying climax. Players feel their choices carry weight.

Weak plots feel contrived, with villains artificially sticking around. Unexpected gotchas happen without setup. The story drags past its natural conclusion. Avoid weak plots that you find yourself forcing forward, even after it should have ended earlier.

*Somehow Palpatine returned, again...*

For example, if the players destroy the villain's secret factory, don't pull out a second one they didn't know about. With no foreshadowing, it feels forced just to extend the plot. Let the victory feel earned. Failures should have consequences too. When a plot reaches its organic end, wrap it up in a narratively fulfilling way.

An immersive plot arises from player agency and impactful choices. Clever twists and strong pacing keep them engaged. Thematic depth provides food for thought. If the villain loses, let the players savor their victory; there are plenty of other villains out there.

## How to Introduce a Villain

When introducing a villain in your campaign, you can broadly classify the methods into two categories: Direct and Subtle.

### Direct Introduction

This approach puts the villain front and center early on. Methods can include:

- **Direct Confrontation.** The party might face off against the villain or their minions in combat.  
- **Witnessing Evil.** Players directly observe the villain's malicious actions or plans.  
- **The Apocalyptic Stack.** My personal favorite. The stakes rapidly escalate into chaos. After taking a few sessions to establish the setting and its characters, unleash the villain as a cataclysmic force, causing successive disasters.  

To make a direct introduction effective, it's essential to ensure the villain's actions have immediate and tangible consequences. The more drastic and personal these consequences are to the party, the stronger their motivation is to oppose the villain.

#### Example

Imagine a scenario where the players ally with local nobility. As they investigate a mysterious kidnapping, they're drawn to a particular noble's mansion. However, they're caught, arrested by a militia under the noble's command, and falsely accused of a crime. As they languish in prison, a beloved NPC tries to rescue them but is executed in front of their eyes by the aristocrat. To add insult to injury, the noble might even cut the hand of a party member—a punishment fitting for thieves. These successive, dire events will make the party despise this cruel noble.

![](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/books/steinhardts-guide-to-the-eldritch-hunt/img/04888.webp#center)

### Subtle Introduction

This is a more indirect way of hinting at the villain's presence or influence in the world. Common techniques include:

- **Rumors.** The party might overhear whispers of a menacing figure or dark deeds.  
- **Aftermaths.** Discovering the results of the villain's actions, like a devastated village or a series of sinister rituals.  
- **Slow Build-up.** Gradually provide clues and incidents that hint at a larger threat. This approach allows you to create a mounting sense of dread and gives players multiple reasons to confront the villain.  

The key to a subtle introduction is patience. Layer the hints, and let the party's understanding of the threat grow organically, increasing their motivation to act against the villain over time.

#### Example:

Within the majestic walls of the Grand Cathedral, famed for its divine hymns, an unsettling silence begins to permeate. As the party delves deeper into the cathedral's daily rituals, they gather whispered accounts: an elf recounts a forbidden song said to be wielded by a banished choirmaster named Seraphel; a concerned priest shares confessions of congregants haunted by nightmares of this very figure, commanding an oppressive silence; and a local merchant hesitantly speaks of neighboring homes that grew silent, with whispers blaming Seraphel's vengeful return. These disparate tales, coupled with the unnerving absence of sacred songs, subtly point to the looming shadow of Seraphel, suggesting he's the malevolent force now echoing through the silent halls of the Grand Cathedral.

## Making Steinhardt Villains

Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt is a mix of gothic and Lovecraftian horror. Because the monsters are typically more Lovecraftian and don't have a particular reason to be evil—their existence alone is a threat—villains in this setting best fit the gothic theme. Regardless of the style, villains tend to fit into three categories.

Below are each of the categories alongside some villain templates you can use.

### 1. Manipulators of Public Perception and Influence

#### _The Propagandist:_


- Uses media and public opinion against the party  
- Motivated by conflicting desires for the population  
- Might resort to slander if opposed  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be a priest of the Radiant Church vying for power, or a member of the Victorian Masonia.*

#### _The Bishop:_


- Leader of a church or cult with strong influence over the populace  
- Might declare excommunication against foes  
- Dark secret behind their faith  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be a priest of the Radiant Church who fell to a dark power from any of the Districts.*

#### _Prince Charming:_


- Appears friendly but uses deceit for personal gain  
- Seeks political power, possibly through marriage  
- Has assets like court influence, wealth, and soldiers  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be queen Victoria Silverblood or Arvidt Silverblood.*

#### _The Corporate Shill:_


- Represents a corporation exploiting locals  
- Operates within legal boundaries but in immoral ways  
- Potentially backed by vast resources and influence  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Any key leader of the Scions or the Warden of the Abyssal Keep.*

### 2. Threats Through Physical or Magical Means

#### _The Trapper:_


- Designs dangerous traps, which they let loose on structures or people  
- Leaves a mark or sigil on their creations  
- Aims to protect something valuable with these traps, or wants to teach people a lesson (think Riddler from 'The Batman')  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be a member of the Victorian Masonia or a demented Scion.*

#### _The Vigilante:_


- Uses physical prowess enhanced by magical items or means  
- Seeks recognition for deeds, not heritage  
- Is convinced they are doing the right thing  
- Might interfere with the party's plans due to their own justice agenda  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be the members of the Unseen Claw or the Forsaken.*

#### _The Pursuer:_


- An unstoppable force constantly chasing the party  
- Single-minded goal (e.g., revenge or artifact retrieval)  
- Appears at the worst possible moments  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be a devouring belua or a crazed hunter.*

### 3. Malevolent Entities and unexpected Threats

#### _The Monkey's Paw:_


- Malevolent being that grants and twists wishes  
- Misinterprets wishes to create chaos or trap players  
- Players might need to defeat them to escape their realm  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be the Defiled Librarian or a Dreamer's avatar*

#### _The Unstable:_


- Sudden magical powers lead to accidental havoc  
- Unpredictable actions and consequences  
- Represents a wildcard threat due to their lack of control  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be a Lightning Vessel or Earthbreaker barbarian, or Abaddon, the failed sentinel.*

#### _The Mutant:_


- Former ally now transformed into a threat  
- Transformation is typically due to a curse (e.g., the Scourge)  
- Dual nature: tries to hide the curse but might succumb to its evil  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be Vicar Priscilla or a Gerhard, though many other of the fallen hunters fit this category*

#### _Lovecraftian Entity:_


- Ancient and incomprehensible being from beyond the known universe  
- Its mere presence or awakening threatens reality itself  
- Induces madness, despair, and existential dread in those who encounter it or its influences  
- Operates on motives and plans beyond human understanding  
- Might have cultists or followers trying to awaken or please it  

*In Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: Could be any aberration tag of CR 10 or higher found in the book—my personal favorites are the Goat-Mother or Orr.*
