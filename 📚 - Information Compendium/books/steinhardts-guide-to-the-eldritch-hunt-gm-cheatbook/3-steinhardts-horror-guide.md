## Introduction

There is a place where the shadows stretch a little longer, where the wind howls with the whispers of the damned, and where the very fabric of reality threatens to unravel, revealing the incomprehensible horrors that lurk just beyond our understanding.

Welcome to the enthralling domain of *Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt*.

Gothic horror, with its tragic tales, haunted mansions, and cursed bloodlines, taps into our deepest fears about our past and the inescapable legacies we might leave behind. Eldritch horror, on the other hand, challenges our understanding of the universe, presenting cosmic entities and phenomena so beyond our comprehension that mere exposure to them can drive a person to madness.

But why introduce these elements into a campaign? The answer lies in the atmosphere and tension they create. While traditional adventures offer excitement, challenges, and epic tales of heroism, adding layers of horror can transform a campaign into a deeply immersive and emotionally charged experience. It's not just about battling monsters; it's about battling one's own fears, confronting the unknown, and facing the darker aspects of the world and oneself.

This book aims to guide Game Masters on a journey into the heart of darkness, providing tools, techniques, and insights to craft a memorable horror-infused campaign. Drawing inspiration from classic literature, iconic horror films, and the lore of *Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt*, we'll delve deep into the mechanics of fear, the nuances of narrative tension, and the art of creating a truly haunting atmosphere.

So, gather your courage, light a candle, and prepare to step into a world where every corner holds a mystery, every shadow might conceal a threat, and every decision can mean the difference between sanity and madness.

## 1. How to Create Horror

If you want to create horror in your tabletop roleplaying games (TTRPG), of all the chapters in the book, make sure to read this one.

Horror, as a genre, has captivated audiences for centuries, from ancient tales whispered around campfires to modern cinematic masterpieces. At the heart of this understanding are the Four Fears. *(There was an entire booklet written by Ash Law about this topic called the "Trajectory of Fear", but let me make a brief summary here.)*

### The Four Fears

We can break down fear in TTRPGs into **drumroll** four pillars of horror.

#### 0. Normality

Our baseline—the world is a normal place with nothing scary in it. You walk down the streets, people are talking, birds are chirping, and everything is as it should be. This is not a pillar, this is a typical noncombat day in a roleplaying game.

#### 1. Unease

The first pillar of horror and the subtlest of the fears, unease is the nagging feeling that something isn't quite right. Though subtle, it is perhaps the most important fear on this list. Movies can get away with cheap jumpscares to get a fight-or-flight response out of their audience, but we can't.

Unease is the whisper of the wind through a broken window, the distant howl of a wolf, or the fleeting glimpse of a figure in the periphery of your vision. In Steinhardt's Guide, this could manifest as a street that's just a bit too quiet or an NPC who avoids direct eye contact for no discernible reason. It's the initial seed of doubt that makes players question their surroundings.

##### Example:

The party enters a tavern where the local drunkards are friendly, but there's an odd tradition they all adhere to—all lights must be extinguished after dark, and no one will say why.

#### 2. Dread

Building on unease, dread is a more focused and immediate fear. It's the realization that the source of your unease is close and might pose a threat. In the context of 5e, dread could be the feeling players get when they notice that the same corpse seems to appear wherever they go, or when they find an abandoned campsite with signs of a struggle but no bodies.

Dread can turn out to be nothing, a mere coincidence, and defused back into unease.

##### Example:

As night falls in the aforementioned tavern, the party hears distant, mournful wailing. Searching for its source, they find an old well adjacent to the tavern, but the wailing stops as they approach, replaced by an oppressive silence.

#### 3. Terror

Terror is the anticipation of horror. It's the most potent type of fear because of its immediacy. It's the pounding of your heart when you hear footsteps behind you in a dark alley, or the chill that runs down your spine when you realize you're not alone in a supposedly empty house.

> [!note] Important Note:
> 
> As H.P Lovecraft once said, "The oldest and strongest emotion of mankind is fear, and the oldest and strongest kind of fear is fear of the unknown." Simply put, if you want to keep your players heart rate up, keep the description vague. The players' imaginations do a better job of scaring them than any description or illustration ever could.
^important-note

##### Example:

The party decides to investigate the well in daylight. As they lower a lantern into its depths, they see fleeting glimpses of pale, ghostly faces rushing up towards them, but they vanish just before reaching the surface.

#### 4. Horror

The culmination of the previous fears, horror is the confrontation with the source of your fear. It's the monster lunging from the shadows or the revelation of the killer's identity. However, it's also the weakest of the fears in terms of lasting impact, especially in a TTRPG format. Once the threat is revealed, the tension is released. This might be the climactic battle with the undead rising from the well or the confrontation with a treacherous NPC.

##### Example:

The party returns to the tavern at night. Despite the warnings, they create light, and the wailing begins again as the spirits of those who were thrown into the well rise, seeking vengeance on the living.

### How to Escalate Fear

Fear, much like a finely tuned instrument, requires a delicate balance. If mishandled or overemphasized, the atmosphere can quickly shift from genuinely terrifying to unintentionally comedic or grotesque. This balance is best understood by examining the potential pitfalls of overindulging in each type of fear.

- **Overdoing Unease.** If everything in your game setting feels out of place or odd, the result can resemble the quirky and comedic atmosphere of The Addams Family. While entertaining, it doesn't evoke genuine dread. The world becomes too predictably unpredictable, and players quickly adapt to the constant oddities.  
- **Overdoing Dread.** If every session introduces a new ominous threat without resolution or escalation, the campaign can devolve into a repetitive "monster hunter" format. The sense of impending doom loses its potency when it becomes the norm.  
- **Overdoing Terror.** Constantly bombarding players with imminent threats without giving them a chance to process or react can turn the game into a joke. The tension becomes unsustainable, and the fear dissipates in the face of relentless, over-the-top threats.  
- **Overdoing Horror.** If the game focuses too heavily on graphic and gruesome revelations, it risks becoming a gratuitous gore-fest. The shock value of horror diminishes with overuse, and players can become desensitized. (As a side-note: I've had entire sessions of the game taking place in an absolute gore-fest, and it can be very fun. Just make sure not to have the entire campaign take place in a lake of blood and guts if you want to inspire horror).  

To effectively harness the power of fear in a TTRPG campaign, it's crucial to maintain a rhythmic balance.

This trajectory should involve slow build-ups, with cycles between normality, unease, and dread. This dance of anticipation keeps players engaged, allowing them to savor the unsettling atmosphere and become deeply invested in the narrative. After multiple cycles, when players have been sufficiently toyed with, and their anticipation is at its peak, it's time to unleash the full force of terror, followed by the climactic revelation of horror.

By understanding and respecting this cycle, Game Masters can craft a horror experience that is both thrilling and emotionally resonant, ensuring that players remain on the edge of their seats.

### Example Horror Set-up: The Gala of Shadows

Here is an example of a scenario you can use for Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt that showcases the four pillars of fear in action.

#### Arrival (Normality):

The party arrives at the grand mansion, greeted by the sight of elegantly dressed guests mingling in the gardens. Soft music plays in the background, and the setting sun casts a golden hue over the scene. It's a picture of opulence and sophistication.

#### The Invitation (Unease):

Upon presenting their invitations, the party notices the peculiar metallic scent of the ink. The date of the gala, "the third night when the moon hides behind clouds," seems oddly specific. The doorman, while polite, has a distant, almost vacant look in his eyes.

#### The Mingling (Normality):

Inside, the characters are swept up in the festivities. They're introduced to local nobles, merchants, and other adventurers. The music is lively, the food is delicious, and for a moment, the earlier unease is forgotten.

#### The Missing Children (Dread):

As the evening progresses, a party member realizes that there are no children present, despite hearing their faint laughter from somewhere in the mansion. When this is inquired about, guests either avoid the question or seem genuinely puzzled, as if they hadn't noticed the absence.

#### The Dance (Normality):

A grand waltz begins, and the characters are invited to dance. The music is enchanting, and the dance floor is a swirl of colors and movement. It's a moment of joy and celebration, and the earlier sense of dread is momentarily pushed aside.

#### The Locked Rooms (Unease):

Curious about the source of the children's laughter, a party member tries to explore the mansion. They find certain rooms locked, with guards posted outside who politely but firmly deny entry. The laughter seems to come from behind these locked doors, but it's distant and echoey, as if from far below.

![](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/books/steinhardts-guide-to-the-eldritch-hunt/img/09584.webp#center)

#### The Toast (Normality):

The host, still unseen, sends out a message thanking everyone for attending and promises a night to remember. A toast is proposed, and everyone drinks to good health and prosperity.

#### The Odd Performers (Dread):

A performance begins in the mansion's central hall. Acrobats and jesters entertain the crowd, but their costumes are unsettling, resembling spiders and webs. Their performance tells a story of a creature from the depths that rises every decade to join the world above, but the ending is left ominously open.

#### The Midnight Announcement (Unease):

As midnight approaches, an announcement is made about a special performance by the host. The guests are abuzz with anticipation, but there's an underlying tension. Whispers circulate about the last gala, a decade ago, and guests who were never seen again.

#### The Crawling Chaos (Terror):

The grand ballroom's ambiance shifts dramatically. The once lavish room seems to decay in real-time, with walls peeling and chandeliers rusting. The silvery light turns a sickly green, casting grotesque shadows. From the grand staircase, a cacophony of skittering sounds emerges, growing louder and more frantic. The sound is everywhere; it erupts from the walls and the floor. The sound is in front of you, around you...it is behind you.

#### The Reveal (Horror):

Then, in a horrifying reveal, the host emerges: not a singular entity, but a writhing mass of spider-like limbs, each ending in the face of a child, their expressions frozen in eternal screams. The central body, where all these limbs converge, is a grotesque fusion of human and arachnid, with multiple gleaming eyes and a gaping maw dripping with a thick, dark ichor. The room's temperature doesn't just drop; it feels like the very life is being sucked out of it.

The creature speaks, but not with one voice. The children's faces, still alive and aware, cry out in a haunting chorus, their voices filled with pain and despair, "Welcome to our eternal gala. You will join us. You will become one with us." The realization hits the party: the host doesn't just devour children; it absorbs them, adding them to its horrifying form and trapping their souls in a state of perpetual torment.

> [!note] Note:
> 
> As you can see by reading the example, the moment where the tensions are the highest are right before the reveal of the monster. Though a gruesome being, the moment before the host's description is far more terrifying. Now it has an AC and Hit Points, and hopefully for your players, they have steel and gunpowder at the ready.
^note

#### Aftermath (Unease):

The party emerges forever changed. Though they were victorious, the cost weighs heavy. The opulent mansion is now cold and empty, with no signs of the gala remaining. Only the memories persist: sacrifice, courage, carnage, and the scars on their psyche. A brighter future awaits, for today a true horror was slain. Either that or they all died.

By oscillating between moments of normality, unease, and dread, the tension is built up gradually. The party is kept on their toes, never quite sure what to expect next, creating an atmosphere of anticipation and suspense, before the sudden escalation into dread and terror.

## 2. Gothic Horror

In this chapter, I'll give you the tools necessary to dive into the heart of gothic horror, examining its foundational pillars and illustrating how to weave them seamlessly into your campaign—everything from the atmosphere to the themes, the characters, and the mysteries.

By the chapter's end, you'll be ready to craft a gothic horror campaign that lingers in your players' memories, long after the dice have stopped rolling. But first things first, let's define gothic horror; it's hard to create something if we don't know what we are creating.

### The Essence of Gothic Horror

Gothic horror is a genre that intertwines the elements of fear, death, and romance into a seamless tapestry. It is characterized by its eerie settings, such as decaying castles or monasteries, and a mood that often hinges on melancholy, mystery, and the supernatural. Sounds familiar? It probably does if you've read Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt.

In the context of TTRPGs, gothic horror challenges the players not just with physical threats but with psychological ones—testing their resolve, sanity, and moral compass.

### Why should you run a gothic horror campaign in 5e?

The reasons are manifold. Gothic horror invites a deeper emotional engagement, allowing players and their characters to confront not just external foes, but internal demons, be they guilt, obsession, or forbidden desires. This genre pushes the boundaries, forcing characters and players alike to grapple with moral complexities and personal dilemmas.

Let's be honest; these themes make for one hell of a story. You probably wouldn't want to play that with 5-year-old kids, but if you like more mature themes, which I assume you do since you are reading this book, you'll feel right at home.

### I. Atmosphere and Setting

The gothic horror genre thrives on settings that are as much characters as they are backdrops. Whether it's a sprawling mansion, a dilapidated cathedral, or an ancient library, these structures are imbued with histories, secrets, and often, haunting monsters.

#### Creating an Eerie Environment

The first tool in establishing a gothic horror setting is the effective use of descriptive language to engage the players' senses. As a GM, your words should paint a vivid picture: the chill of a ghostly breeze, the oppressive gloom of an ancient crypt, or the unsettling silence of a forsaken village. Use descriptions to create an atmosphere that not only sets the visual scene, but also touches upon smell, sound, and even the emotional ambiance of a location.

#### Tips for Descriptive Engagement

- **Invoke the Senses.** Detail not just what the players see but what they hear, smell, and feel. The rustle of dry leaves, the scent of mold in the air, or the cold dampness of stone walls can all be used to create a fuller sensory experience.  
- **Elaborate.** Rather than simply informing the players that a place is frightening, describe elements that naturally evoke fear or unease, such as the flickering shadows cast by a dying candle or the unsettling creaks and groans of an old house. Instead of just saying a room is "cold," describe how a chill seems to seep into the bones, making the characters' breath visible.  
- **Foreshadowing.** Use the environment to hint at future events or the history of the setting, like claw marks on the walls or an inexplicably locked door, to build anticipation and suspense.  

#### Gothic Locations

The quintessential gothic horror setting is one that is both beautiful and haunting—a reflection of the duality found within the genre itself.

##### _A Guide to Iconic Gothic Settings:_


If you play using Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt, most locations have an associated mystery or tragedy. A haunted castle might have been the site of a brutal betrayal. An abandoned asylum could whisper secrets of inhumane treatments, and patients may still linger as restless spirits. Thoroughly reading the lore of each location in the first part of the book should supply you with ideas that you can use for your own brand of gothic horror.

In case you wish to add your own, here's a list of some of the most common locations you can use for gothic horror. I used this list myself when writing Steinhardt's Guide.

- **Crumbling Castles.** Imposing structures with dark histories and hidden secrets.  
- **Abandoned Asylums.** Institutions filled with the remnants of past horrors and madness.  
- **Ancient Crypts and Cemeteries.** Resting places for the dead, often disturbed or haunted.  
- **Fog-laden Forests.** Dense woods where the line between the natural and supernatural blurs.  
- **Decadent Mansions.** Opulent homes that house tragic pasts and spectral inhabitants.  
- **Forsaken Villages.** Isolated communities with superstitious and mysterious traditions.  
- **Gloomy Moors.** Expanses of untamed land, often the sites of eerie phenomena or monstrous creatures.  
- **Ruined Monasteries.** Religious sites defiled by pagan rituals or unholy presences.  
- **Gothic Cathedrals.** Grand religious edifices that hide ancient secrets and forbidden knowledge.  
- **Derelict Factories.** Abandoned industrial sites, often the scene of tragic accidents and hauntings.  
- **Desolate Lighthouses.** Solitary structures plagued by tales of shipwrecks and drowned sailors.  
- **Victorian Townhouses.** Urban dwellings that conceal sinister events behind a veneer of respectability.  
- **Clifftop Castles.** Perilous locations battered by winds and waves, harboring legends of old.  
- **The Old Library.** Repositories of forbidden books and knowledge that should not be sought.  
- **The Opera House.** A place of high culture shadowed by dramatic tragedies and ghostly performers.  
- **The Walled Garden.** Overgrown and forgotten, hiding its own brand of natural malice or tragedy.  
- **Sunken Ships.** Underwater wrecks that are the domain of drowned souls and lost treasures.  
- **The Crossroads.** Places outside the boundaries of normal society, where deals with dark forces are struck.  
- **Subterranean Tunnels.** Networks beneath the earth that hold ancient secrets and perilous creatures.  
- **Haunted Inns.** Rest stops that offer no rest, with each room holding its own tale of horror.  

#### Weather and Time

The manipulation of weather and the time of day is a powerful method to enhance the mood of your setting.

- **Weather.** A sudden fog can disorient the players and hide lurking dangers. A storm can trap the players in a haunted location, and the thunder mirrors the tension within. A blizzard can serve as the backdrop upon which streaks of werewolf blood will be drawn.  

    Most importantly, weather must add to the unease by muddling the senses—most often sight. A monster will be far more terrifying if its form is partially hidden in the fog, rather than if it is seen in broad daylight.  
- **Time of Day.** The setting of the sun and the rise of the moon can change the nature of a location. The benign town square by day could become a hunting ground for beasts by night. Twilight can serve as a time when the veil between the living and the dead grows thin. This genre is about change and the horrors it can bring; changes from light to dark can mirror that feeling in your games.  

### II. The Themes

The essence of gothic horror is not just in its setting, but in the themes that run like a chilling draft through the narratives. Below is a list of the themes that make gothic horror what it is. You don't have to use all of them, they are merely here to be snagged for your own use.

- **Mysteries of the Past.** One of the most common gothic horror tropes. Allow the characters to uncover ancient secrets and confront the sins of the past. Whether it's a curse afflicting a bloodline or the discovery of an ancestral crime, these elements create a compelling backstory that haunts the world your players find themselves in.  
- **Emotions.** Encourage your players to dive into their characters' emotions. Gothic horror thrives on feelings of terror, passion, and suspense. Craft situations that elicit strong emotional responses and challenge the characters' psychological limits. Examples of such situations include: being confronted by someone a character wronged in the past, witnessing horrors happening to innocents (children and puppies are my go to), or situations where a character must choose between their own well-being or the lives of others.  
- **Romance.** Dark, forbidden, or doomed romance is a cornerstone of gothic horror. These narratives can be a powerful motivator and a source of dramatic tension, driving the characters to make risky decisions. These romances can occur between NPCs or, for maximum impact, affect the players directly.  
- **The Duality of Human Nature.** Present moral quandaries that explore the characters' inner conflicts and the dual nature of man. Characters should face their own shadows—NPCs that mirror their hidden selves, emphasizing the theme of the duality within us all. Present characters with options that let them feel justified in their rage, and then pull the rug from under them when the NPC that once followed them now refuses to talk to them, as they've become no different than a beast.  
- **Nature as a Force.** The natural world is often depicted as a powerful force that can mirror or enhance the tumultuous emotions and events of the narrative. Nature is no helpless maiden, but rather a destructive force that brings about plagues and floods. The Circle of Symbiosis in Steinhardt's Guide can be used to reflect the destructive element of the natural world.  
- **The Struggle Against Fate.** Pose the question of predestination versus free will. Create prophecies and omens that the characters can either submit to or resist, weaving a narrative thread where the struggle against a seemingly inescapable fate is central. If you want inspiration for this, the entire "Berserk" manga explores this theme.  
- **The Specter of Death.** Make mortality a clear and present danger. The ever-looming presence of death should be a pervasive element, whether through a character's illness, the threat of a malevolent creature, or the decay of the world around them. Don't be afraid to put characters on a clock that will spell their doom if not acted upon quickly.  
- **The Decay of Morality.** Illustrate the corruption of the human soul through NPCs and scenarios that showcase the decline into depravity. Present the characters with temptations that test their moral compass. Perhaps a hunter who indulges too much in bloodshed or a noble who sinks to the bottom of the sins of the flesh.  
- **The Supernatural.** Ghosts, vampires, monsters, and other supernatural beings are staples of gothic horror, often serving as metaphors for the unknown or the human psyche. You can open up the bestiary of Steinhardt's Guide and pick any monster that doesn't look eldritch, and you'll have yourself a plot device for your gothic needs.  
- **Science and Transgression.** Characters in gothic horror often grapple with the consequences of overreaching knowledge or playing God, particularly in the realm of science, and much like the myth of Icarus, flying this close to the sun isn't without its consequences. The Scions are the perfect example of that.  
- **The Isolation of the Individual.** Most gothic horror stories, such as the classic 'Dracula' by Bram Stoker, take place in isolated locations. Don't be afraid to put the characters in your campaign in isolation, whether physically separated from society in a desolate manor or emotionally distanced due to their experiences. This isolation can lead to a greater sense of vulnerability, and in turn greater stories.  
- **The Grotesque.** Characters or settings that are bizarre, malformed, or unsettling are used to evoke fear and challenge conventional standards. It can range from mechanical beings that act almost as humans to complete freaks, like the one portrayed in Frankenstein.  
- **Religion and Morality.** Gothic horror often critiques or explores religious themes, with characters confronting moral dilemmas in the context of their religious beliefs. If you have players who play religious characters, don't hesitate to put their beliefs to the test, and inversely, if you have atheist characters, create scenarios that test the depth of their lack of faith.  
- **The Erosion of Reality.** Introduce surreal elements that blur the line between what's real and what's imagined. Dreams, hallucinations, and eerie coincidences can undermine the characters' sense of reality, leading them to question everything they perceive. We will delve into this more in the Lovecraftian horror section of this book.  

By integrating some or all of these themes, your narrative will breathe with the oppressive atmosphere we are looking for.

#### Plot and Subplot Ideas

When you're spinning a web of gothic horror in your campaign, the main plot and subplots are the silk threads that ensnare your players. Here's some common gothic threads you can use to weave a tapestry of terror and intrigue. Encourage your players to engage with these ideas, and they'll find themselves in a story full of dread, discovery, and the darkness that dwells in the human heart.

These are pulled from Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt, but they should give a solid framework, should you choose to make your own. You'll notice that each of these plots uses one or more of the themes mentioned above.

##### _Main Plot Ideas:_


- **The Curse of the Silver Bloodline.** The characters are embroiled in a tale where an ancestral royal curse plagues the land. They must uncover the origins of the curse and find a way to break it before they become the next victims.  
- **The Haunting.** A malignant headless horseman haunts the Ruby District, and the characters must uncover the ghost's tragic history of corruption within the Circle of Symbiosis to put it to rest. This could involve a mixture of investigation, diplomacy with restless spirits, and the cleansing of corrupted forests.  
- **The Madness that Spreads.** A mysterious affliction called the Scourge drives inhabitants of the secluded city-state of Luyarnha to madness, turning them into beasts. The characters must find the source of this insanity, whether it's a malevolent force, a contagious malady, or the influence of a dark deity.  
- **The Forbidden Knowledge.** The characters come into possession of a book that holds forbidden knowledge of Osteomancy. They must choose between safeguarding this power or using it, all while evading those who seek to claim it for their nefarious purposes.  
- **The Unholy Pact.** A powerful figure of the Church has made a deal with a dark entity, and the repercussions are beginning to surface. The characters must navigate a web of political intrigue and supernatural horror to expose or thwart the pact.  

##### _Subplot Ideas:_


- **The Lost Traveler.** The characters encounter a traveler who is searching for a lost loved one, leading the party into the depths of a cursed forest or the abandoned wing of a castle, revealing a smaller, more personal tragedy within the larger narrative.  
- **The Eerie Portent.** Characters witness omens that foretell a lunar apocalypse. Investigating these portents can lead them to hidden cults, ancient prophecies, or unexpected alliances with characters who have their own secret agendas.  
- **The Relic Hunt.** In their travels, the characters learn of relics that are said to ward off evil or break curses, such as the one plaguing the youngest of the royal Silverblood family. Retrieving these items can involve braving haunted crypts, bargaining with eccentric collectors, or performing forbidden rituals.  
- **The Doppelganger.** A character's exact double appears, sowing chaos and mistrust. This subplot can lead to an exploration of identity, illusion, and the supernatural, forcing the characters to question their own perceptions and allegiances.  
- **The Disgraced Noble.** A noble has been ousted from society due to a scandal or accusation of crime. The characters can help to restore the noble's honor, or perhaps discover that the noble is the linchpin in a larger, darker plot that threatens the city.  

By mixing and matching these plot ideas, you can create a complex narrative that allows for both the grand scale of gothic horror and the intimate, personal horror that can be just as unsettling.

#### Mystery and Investigation

In gothic horror, mystery is not just an element of the story; it is the shroud that envelops everything and the urge compelling the players to peer closer into the darkness. It's the protagonist slowly unravelling the nature of Count Dracula or the maiden discovering her family's curse. Below are the main cornerstones to building good mystery and investigation arcs in your campaign.

- **The Art of the Unseen.** Begin by presenting the unknown. Perhaps it's a series of unexplained disappearances, a cryptic prophecy, or a forbidden ritual that's only hinted at in hushed tones. The less the players know upfront, the more enticing the mystery. Create questions that demand answers, but don't make those answers easy to find.  
- **Layered Clues.** Develop a trail of clues that lead players deeper into the gothic atmosphere. A bloodstained letter, a broken family crest, or a series of archaic symbols scratched into a cell's wall can all serve as breadcrumbs. Each clue should feel like a discovery, pulling back a curtain to reveal a new layer of intrigue.  
- **Red Herrings and False Leads.** A good mystery should have its share of dead ends and misdirections. A character who seems suspicious might simply be a red herring, while the true antagonist's machinations remain hidden. This not only adds complexity to the narrative but also heightens the sense of uncertainty and paranoia that is quintessential to gothic horror.  
- **Atmospheric Revelations.** When a clue is uncovered or a secret is revealed, add emphasis to make it memorable. The discovery of a hidden passage should come with a cold gust of wind and the distant sound of a child's lullaby. Finding a long-lost tome should be accompanied by the musty scent of decay and the feeling of unseen eyes watching. These atmospheric touches ensure that the gothic tone permeates every revelation.  
- **Interwoven Histories.** The mysteries in gothic horror often have roots in the past. A haunted location might have a history of tragedy that reflects its current state. A cursed object might be tied to an ancient betrayal. Weave the backstory of your world (and of the player characters) into the mysteries the party is trying to solve to give them a sense of the larger narrative at play.  
- **Interactive Investigation.** Encourage your players to interact with the world as part of their investigation by creating puzzles or other tangible activities. Let them perform seances to speak with the dead, decode cryptic journals for clues, or experiment with alchemical concoctions to reveal hidden messages—but don't make the players drink weird potions! This active participation makes the mysterysolving process more engaging.  
- **The Payoff.** Finally, ensure there's a payoff that's worth the buildup. The resolution of the mystery should bring a mix of horror and satisfaction. Whether it's a tragic tale of lost love leading to a ghost's unrest, or the unveiling of a villain whose malevolence is as grand as it is ghastly, the conclusion should feel earned and impactful.  

Each investigation becomes a thread in the overarching narrative tapestry, one that feels both personal and epic, with the power to chill them to their core.

### III. Characters and Archetypes

#### NPC Creation: Designing Non-Player Characters with Gothic Flair

The denizens of your gothic horror narrative are as crucial as the shadow-draped settings they inhabit. A well-crafted NPC can be an unforgettable highlight of your campaign.

The guiding principle for such NPCs is simple. Pick one of the themes of gothic horror described above and embody it into your NPC. This will help tremendously when you determine their motivations, fears, and desires.

What drives the mad scientist to such lengths? What tragedy befell the tortured noble? As you flesh out these details, consider how they speak, how they move, and how they react to the players. Perhaps the doomed lover only whispers, or the vengeful ghost has a chilling wail that precedes its appearances.

You don't need to go into such depth for each NPC you create, but extra thought should be given for main villains or NPCs central to your campaign. Let's dive into some examples:

- **The Tortured Noble.** A noble, seemingly bathed in wealth and privilege, but cursed with a hidden affliction. This could be a literal curse or a metaphorical one, such as a dark family secret. Their demeanor should oscillate between haughty arrogance and profound despair, and their manor should be a reflection of their psyche—grandiose yet decaying.  
- **The Mad Scientist.** This archetype, driven by insatiable curiosity, tramples over ethical boundaries. When designing this character, focus on their obsession—whether it's reanimating the dead, unlocking the secrets of the human mind, or transcending nature itself. Their laboratory is a treasure trove of narrative hooks: morally dubious experiments, strange creatures in jars, and dangerous inventions gone awry.  
- **The Doomed Lover.** Gothic horror is ripe with tales of love that defy the bounds of mortality. The doomed lover is trapped in a cycle of loss and yearning, perhaps pining for a ghost or seeking to resurrect a deceased paramour. Their sorrow is a heavy cloak, and it might lead them to seek the aid of the players or, in desperation, turn to darker powers.  
- **The Vengeful Ghost.** Not all NPCs need be of the flesh. A specter, bound by vengeance, can provide a wealth of story possibilities. Was their death a tragic accident or a nefarious murder? Their interactions with the party might be cryptic and fraught with sorrow, their appearances sudden and terrifying. Their existence may be the result of our above-mentioned doomed lover's action.  
- **The Mysterious Wanderer.** Cloaked in enigma, this character flits on the periphery of the party's journey. Perhaps they are an immortal being, or a traveler with knowledge of the arcane. Their motives are obscure, and their guidance is a double-edged sword—useful yet potentially misleading.  

#### Player Characters: Guidance for Creating Protagonists in a Gothic Horror Campaign

In the mist-shrouded realm of gothic horror, the player characters are the beating hearts within the body of the narrative. Here's how to guide your players in crafting protagonists that resonate with the gothic horror aesthetic.

##### _Embracing the Gothic Ethos:_


- **Touched by Darkness.** Encourage players to consider how the dark themes of gothic horror have touched their characters' pasts. Perhaps they have a mysterious lineage, a haunting guilt, or a tragic loss that drives their quest. This element of darkness should be a well from which they draw strength and motivation, not just a source of endless sorrow.  
- **Complex Morality.** Encourage players to think beyond the dichotomy of good and evil. Gothic protagonists often walk the line of moral ambiguity and are driven by complex motivations. A character might be pushed to do questionable things in the name of love, revenge, or survival. If one player wants to play a purely good or entirely evil character, they will serve as a stark moral contrast against the rest of the party.  
- **Burning Passions.** Gothic horror is steeped in the intense emotions of Romanticism. Encourage players to imbue their characters with a sense of passion—be it for a person, an ideal, or a quest for knowledge. This passion should be a driving force in their story, pushing them into dramatic and dangerous situations, and sometimes pushing their moral compass to the wayside.  

##### _Guiding Character Creation:_


- **Backgrounds with Depth.** Assist players in developing backgrounds that provide a springboard for future storylines. A character might have an ancestral home that is now haunted, or they may be seeking redemption for a past misdeed. These backgrounds should open doors to further exploration within the campaign. Feel free to plunder the ones listed in Steinhardt's Guide and tie them to other events in the lore of your campaign.  
- **Flaws and Fears.** Gothic heroes are often flawed, and their fears can be as compelling as their strengths. Some players have the tendency of wanting to play an infallible character, but flaws are what give depth to the character. I'm not going to tell you to force your players to choose flaws, but I'm certainly not going to punish you if you do. These flaws should come into play in meaningful ways, such as a fear of darkness in a campaign filled with shadows, or a propensity to fall in love with the wrong person, creating a web of personal conflict.  
- **Skills and Abilities.** While character skills should be useful, they also need to fit within the gothic theme. Work with your players to decide what minor abilities their combination of skills and background would grant them. A bard might specialize in mournful dirges that summon spirits via religion checks, or a wizard could be an expert at deciphering ancient family curses via investigation checks. Encourage creativity in how abilities are themed and described.  

> [!note] Note—Collaborative Storytelling:
> 
> Remind your players that they are co-authors in the campaign's unfolding story. Their characters' actions, decisions, and growth will contribute to the story you're collectively weaving. Before your first session, encourage them to think about how their personal tales might intersect with the larger narrative, with the stories of their fellow adventurers, and with the goals they would like their character to achieve.

#### Antagonists and Monsters: Crafting Foes Worthy of Gothic Horror

Antagonists and monsters are not merely obstacles to be overcome; they are integral to the tapestry of terror and tragedy we are trying to reach. They should embody the themes of the genre, often serving as dark reflections of the protagonists or as manifestations of the world's corrupting influence.

Later in the book, I detail how to create great villains, taking you step by step. Here I'll focus on the seasoning—the flavor needed for things to feel right at home in your gothic setting.

A gothic antagonist is rarely evil for evil's sake. They are characters with histories, motives, and perhaps a shred of sympathy. Often they are villains that have fallen from grace or are driven by a warped sense of love, vengeance, or desire for knowledge. Their complexity should challenge players' preconceptions and elicit a mix of dread and empathy.

Gothic monsters often symbolize deeper themes, such as the fear of the unknown or the consequences of playing God. When designing creatures, consider the themes of gothic horror we mentioned above. A vampire might embody the perils of eternal life, while a ghost could represent unresolved guilt. Let's dive into some examples:

- **Cursed Beings.** Some antagonists may be victims of a curse or a dark pact that has twisted them into monstrous forms. These characters can be tragic figures that struggle with their loss of humanity, serving as a cautionary tale and adding depth to the narrative.  
- **Madness and Obsession.** Many gothic horrors feature a protagonist turned antagonist through madness or obsession. Create NPCs who have been consumed by their quest for forbidden knowledge, unrequited love, or revenge. Bonus points if the quest that brought them to madness is the same as the one a player character is currently afflicted by. This descent into madness will be a terrifying mirror for the character, warning them of the dangers of the paths they tread.  
- **Eliciting Fear and Wonder.** Gothic horror is about eliciting a sense of fear punctuated by moments of wonder. Design creatures that are not only frightening in appearance but also in the implications of their existence. An encounter with a monster should be an encounter with the uncanny, something that shakes the players' understanding of the world. Encountering a lycan in an orphanage is not only terrifying because of the lycan, but it now means that the curse—or a fate far worse—has also spread to innocent children.  
- **Unearthly Origins.** Gothic creatures are often not just natural beasts but have origins steeped in the supernatural or the blasphemous. They might be the result of a dark ritual gone awry, the spawn of a deity's wrath, or remnants of a long-forgotten civilization. Their otherworldliness should add an aura of mystery and danger to their presence.  

##### _Integrating Characters and Monsters_


The best gothic villains and monsters are woven into the narrative in ways that elevate the entire campaign. They should have personal ties to the locations the characters visit, the stories they uncover, and even to the characters themselves. Perhaps the lycan that slaughters innocents in the city streets is related to one of the player characters, or the mad scientist was once a mentor to one of them.

Creating memorable gothic horror antagonists and monsters is about blending the grotesque with the tragic, the horrific with the human. Your creatures and villains should not only challenge the players in combat but also in their hearts and minds, leaving them with moral quandaries and emotional scars.

#### Gothic Traps and Puzzles

Traps and puzzles are classic gothic horror devices for building suspense and a sense of peril. When designing them, focus more on imagination and atmosphere over mechanical complexity. Save the intricate gears and levers for your standard dungeon; gothic horror is best served by visceral, unsettling obstacles.

For example, a shadowy hallway filled with disturbing noises can transition into a trap where the passageway fills with blood, rising rapidly to choke and drown the party if they don't find a way out quickly. A spacious crypt might have stone walls that slowly squeeze together to crush any still inside—the party must hurry to solve an ominous riddle or find the mechanism to stop it.

Floors or stairs that collapse into chasms filled with grasping corpses are excellent "gotcha" traps, particularly if they have scattered bones nearby as foreshadowing. You can also trap the party in a room where phantasmal killers seem to emerge from the very walls themselves. Provide clues for how to escape, but make it tense with apparitions only some characters can see.

For puzzles, focus on mysteries and moral dilemmas instead of intricate mechanical challenges. Characters might need to reconstruct an unholy ritual to open a door, but doing so has a corrupting effect. Placing an ancient mummified hand in the wrong statue could crumble the floor, forcing the party to risk a dangerous jump.

With some creepy descriptions and imagery, even simple traps and puzzles can become chilling and memorable in a gothic horror adventure. Disturb your players' imaginations rather than relying solely on mechanical challenges. However, avoid making the puzzles or traps too complex to figure out. Players can be very smart, but in my personal experience, when it comes to puzzles, they leave their brain at the door.

## 3. Lovecraftian Horror

### Brief Overview of What Lovecraftian Horror Is

Lovecraftian horror, often referred to as cosmic horror, is a subgenre of horror fiction that emphasizes the cosmic horror of the unknown more than gore or other elements of shock. It is named after American author H.P. Lovecraft, whose work has inspired a vast body of literature and an entire subgenre of horror, emphasizing themes of cosmic dread, forbidden knowledge, the limits of human understanding, and the insignificance of humanity within the vastness of the cosmos.

In Lovecraftian horror, the "monsters" aren't just creatures that go bump in the night; they are often indescribable, incomprehensible cosmic entities. These beings embody terrifying truths—that our knowledge and morals mean nothing on a cosmic scale. The horror comes less from physical danger and more from the existential crisis that comes from confronting these entities and the unsettling truths they represent.

### Why It's a Compelling Choice for TTRPG Campaigns

- **Depth and Complexity.** Lovecraftian horror adds layers of depth and complexity to your campaign. It goes beyond the straightforward good versus evil dichotomy, plunging your players into ethical and existential dilemmas.  
- **Psychological Engagement.** This genre engages players on a psychological level, offering a different kind of tension and suspense. It's not just about surviving physical threats, but also about grappling with unsettling revelations and the characters' own sanity, offering emotional depth and character development opportunities.  
- **Narrative Flexibility.** Lovecraftian elements can be woven into just about any setting and story type, from high fantasy to steampunk, making it a versatile choice for Game Masters.  
- **High Stakes.** The stakes in Lovecraftian horror are often cosmic in scale, adding a sense of urgency and grandeur to your campaign. This can be a refreshing change from more localized, less apocalyptic threats.  
- **Player Agency.** While Lovecraftian horror often emphasizes the insignificance of humanity, it also offers unique opportunities for heroism. The choices players make in the face of incomprehensible cosmic entities can have far-reaching consequences, adding weight and significance to their actions.  
- **Unique Gameplay Mechanics.** Lovecraftian horror offered thematic guidance for new mechanics in *Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt*, like the revamped madness system or the Eldritch Moons, which offer new gameplay mechanics that keep players on their toes.  
- **Enhanced World-Building.** The genre allows for the creation of unique, otherworldly settings and creatures that can make your campaign stand out and be memorable, such as the Nightmare Realms in *Steinhardt's Guide*.  

### Key Elements of Lovecraftian Horror

Incorporating Lovecraftian horror into your campaign requires a nuanced understanding of its key elements. These are the building blocks that will help you create an atmosphere of existential dread and cosmic horror. Below are the essential components to focus on:

#### Cosmic Indifference

A core theme of Lovecraftian horror is the indifference of the cosmos towards human life and their concerns. Lovecraftian entities and forces represent this indifference. They operate according to their own alien drives and motivations, uncaring and unaware of humanity's existence. Leaning into this theme creates an unsettling atmosphere as characters confront these truths. They may do everything right and still get punished because the universe just doesn't care.

#### Personal Insignificance

No matter their worldly status or personal achievements, a single individual is inconsequential compared to the vastness of the cosmos. Protagonists struggle in vain against forces far beyond their comprehension, let alone their influence. As they come to understand their insignificance, characters can feel despair and madness, and might make reckless attempts to make an impact before their inevitable end.

#### Incomprehensible Threats

Central to cosmic horror is the presence of threats that are inconceivable. Lovecraftian entities, cults, magic, and other phenomena offer glimpses and fragments that hint at a greater inscrutable horror. Pushing characters and players to confront something fundamentally unintelligible provides an unsettling gameplay experience. The greatest threats are those that defy easy categorization and explanation.

#### Failing Sanity

Witnessing unimaginable horrors and existential truths beyond understanding strains the fragile mortal mind beyond its limits. Gradually, the psyche erodes, and the characters' sanity becomes compromised. Some may descend into madness and debility, rendering them even more vulnerable.

This looming threat of failing sanity adds pressure and urgency. Even the strongest will or faith offers little protection against threats titanic enough to unravel one's very personality.

The "Madness System in Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt" can help you turn this concept into a reality at your table.

#### Isolation and Helplessness

Characters frequently find themselves alone and helpless against the Lovecraftian threats they face. Whether isolated in remote locations, abandoned by companions, or unable to get others to believe their experience, protagonists often confront the horror singly or in small groups.

Allies may find their knowledge and skills useless against otherworldly forces. Weapons or magic may prove ineffective. Protagonists struggle in vain as their understanding of the world provides little aid. The isolation enhances the helpless feeling.

This sense of hopeless isolation in the face of the unfathomable enhances the existential dread. With no help arriving or means of combating the threat, characters are forced to draw on inner mental reserves to overcome the horror—often the only thing left once all other options are exhausted.

### Lovecraftian Setting

The setting itself is a conduit for horror in Lovecraftian tales. Lean into locales that innately breed unease—abandoned mansions, mist-shrouded villages, cyclopean ruins, and alien vistas. Describe such places vividly through sensory details like fetid smells, bizarre architectural angles, and shadows that move against the light.

> [!note] Note:
> 
> You'll notice that a couple of these examples feel right at home in gothic horror too. This is perfect for you as the GM, as you can pick the direction you want to focus or build one on top of the other.

Use all five senses when describing locations. The smell of long-expired incense clinging to the interior of an abandoned temple. A portrait whose eyes seem to stare back from the shadows. The skittering sounds of unseen movement fading down a hallway. The taste of something foul upon the wind. The feeling of sand so fine that it's like silk beneath one's feet.

> [!quote]  
> 
> As you approach the abandoned mansion, a cloying miasma of decay assaults your nostrils while mournful echoes seem to emanate from within its broken walls. Shattered mirrors reflect your torchlight a thousandfold, their fractured surfaces giving the illusion of being watched by an unseen presence in the dust-choked hallways...

#### Non-Euclidean Geometry

Lovecraftian horror focuses on otherworldly experiences, such non-Euclidean angles, intricate carvings depicting creatures beyond imagination, or pools of stagnant water spreading twisted reflections. These things can be hard to describe to players, so here's a quick list you can refer to for help.

##### Use Comparisons

Compare the shapes and angles to mundane objects the players are familiar with. "The walls seem to tilt and bend at impossible angles, like looking through a twisted funhouse mirror."

##### Emphasize Disorientation

Focus on how the geometry makes the players feel. "The stairs spiral in dizzying configurations, warping in a way that strains your perception and makes your head spin."

##### Use Movement and Senses

Describe how moving through the space, or how the senses are affected, reveals its impossible nature. "As you walk down the corridor, its length seems to shift and contract erratically with each step you take."

##### Lean on Abstractions

Use vivid but abstract descriptions. "The ancient stone columns seem to splash open and melt into the room itself like wax, their edges blurring into the walls and ceiling."

##### Redefine Familiar Terms

Take familiar architectural features like hallways, walls, and ceilings and describe them behaving in contradictory ways. "The hallway slopes away from you as if at an angle, yet you feel level with the ground."

##### Emphasize the Inexplicable

Don't try to explain the geometry logically. Focus on its bizarre wrongness. "You find yourself somehow on the ceiling of the tower with no stairs in sight, defying any rational explanation."

The key is to convey the alien, reality-defying nature of the space through visceral details and imaginative language without getting bogged down in the mathematics and logic behind it—because there is none. Lean on the players' disorientation and confusion to drive home the disturbing atmosphere.

### Moral Ambiguity and Ethical Dilemmas

In Lovecraftian horror, morality is complex and choices are rarely easy. Players frequently face dilemmas where there are no clear good outcomes, only less-bad ones.

#### Present Tough Choices

Design quests that lack obvious right solutions. For example, players may have to:

- Choose whether or not to seal a portal that will trap allies and enemies alike.  
- Decide if they should free a dangerous entity to aid them against a greater threat.  
- Betray an NPC to prevent a worse betrayal later on.  

Have players debate options with ethical implications. Avoid signaling any choice as morally superior.

#### Unpredictable Consequences

After major decisions, introduce surprising consequences that follow logically but aren't expected. For example:

- An NPC's sacrifice causes unintended collateral damage.  
- A freed monster later turns against the players.  
- Helping a faction empowers them to commit evil acts.  

Make cosmic entities react in thematically appropriate but unpredictable ways to reinforce indifference to human morality. They do not think like us, therefore do not act like us.

#### Dilemmas Reveal Characters

Tie dilemmas to characters' backgrounds, beliefs, and ongoing stories for personal stakes. How they react highlights who they are—their principles, motivations, and mental limits.

Maybe a paladin struggles with betraying oaths to prevent a greater evil. Or a warlock is tempted to sacrifice innocents to gain power. The choices characters make in difficult situations showcase development.

#### Morally Complex NPCs

Populate the world with NPCs that challenge moral assumptions, like:

- Zealots willing to cause harm for a "righteous" cause (e.g., The Radiant Church).  
- Villains who believe they are doing the right thing (see the "Villains chapter" of this book).  
- Guides who offer shortcuts that require moral compromise.  
- These NPCs can provide morally gray quests and showcase how values warp into evil.  

By presenting earned quandaries, unpredictable fallout, dilemmas tied to characters, and clashing perspectives via NPCs, you can explore the themes of moral relativism and ethical complexity that define Lovecraftian cosmic horror.

### Creating Existential Fear

The core purpose of incorporating Lovecraftian themes is to evoke a sense of existential fear and dread in players, not merely to provide monsters to defeat. The threats should challenge characters psychologically and philosophically, not just physically.

Focus on pushing characters outside their comfort zones, confronting them with unsettling truths about reality that induce desperation and paranoia. The goal is for players to feel unnerved as their understanding of existence is called into question. Lean into elements like failing sanity, helplessness, and moral ambiguity to generate fear.

#### Impossible to Defeat Entities

The greatest cosmic threats are meant to be impossible to definitively defeat or destroy for good. Characters may disrupt a particular plan or banish them for a time, but the entities eventually reconstitute or return in some form, indifferent and inexorable. Any victory against them is fleeting.

This reinforces the insignificance of humanity—these forces operate on power scales beyond any mortal magic or weapon.

#### Indirect Manipulation

Rather than overtly controlling characters, which isn't very fun in TTRPGs, subtly influence them through dreams, compulsions, and seemingly meaningless coincidences that steer them towards unnerving destinies. Make players paranoid they are being manipulated while keeping the true nature of the forces ambiguous.

#### Sparingly Reveal Lore

Mythos lore should raise more questions than answers. If characters learn anything, it should be fragments that hint at disturbing truths without clear meaning. Never conclusively explain cosmic threats—keep them disturbing and mysterious.

![](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/books/steinhardts-guide-to-the-eldritch-hunt/img/06947.webp#center)

#### Avoid Clean Solutions

Investigation leads to more puzzles and complexity, not simple solutions. The mythos should resist understanding or clean resolutions. Defeating cosmic threats must be temporary, ambiguous, and come at a steep cost. Offer your players a resolution, otherwise it is unsatisfying, but make it obvious that it is an incomplete one.

#### Drive Paranoia

Utilize manipulated coincidences, partial revelations, and maddening lore to make players suspicious of unseen forces controlling destiny. But do not confirm concrete answers, and avoid clean resolutions—let the possibility of being subtly maneuvered by the unfathomable gnaw at characters' sanity. The cosmic mythos should feel fundamentally unknowable.

#### Character Change Over Universe Change

Protagonists in Lovecraftian tales often cannot substantially change or impact the cosmic horrors that threaten reality. But through their struggles and experiences, they are inwardly changed themselves.

The change may be going mad, discovering some revelatory truth about existence, or finding power to temporarily protect their realm against the entity. Since the universe remains indifferent, characters are forced to go through profound personal arcs to overcome the horror.

By keeping threats perpetually looming, limiting decisive victories, and focusing on character growth, you can craft Lovecraftian scenarios that evoke existential fear rather than thrill at monsters being slain—a hallmark of cosmic horror done right.

### Gradually Reveal the Horror

Much like in gothic horror, at first, present a superficially normal setting, then slowly unveil its unsettling elements as players explore. Introduce odd clues like strange sigils, deteriorating log entries, or the frightened ramblings of inhabitants. Lead players to uncover the darker secrets and history buried beneath the surface. Build tension through pacing and revelation rather than immediate and overt horror.

#### NPCs Reflect the Horror

Populate your setting with NPCs that embody its mood. A nervous innkeeper, a cagey scholar of forbidden lore, or eerily silent villagers communicate the disturbance bubbling under the genteel veneer. Their peculiar behaviors, reactions to locations, and veiled dialogues provide interactive hints about the environment's true nature.

#### Empower the Setting

The setting is an active character, not just a container for the action. Link events, reveals, and progress to specific locations and environmental interactions. Make the unraveling mystery integral to the history and identity of the setting itself. This transforms scene details from window dressing into storytelling tools that organically unify setting and narrative.

By making an oppressive atmosphere intrinsic to the world itself, you can immerse players in an unsettling environment that deepens horror through implication and inference rather than obvious frights. You want the players to have a feeling of "We are so fucked" rather than one of screaming terror.

#### Minions Hint at Greater Threats

Lesser monsters and minions can serve a key role: hinting at and foreshadowing the greater otherworldly forces at play. Used creatively, these smaller threats set the stage for the reveal of more significant cosmic horrors. Some examples include:

- Exploring an underground ruin, the party is hounded by wights bearing strange symbols of an alien faith. Their magic implies a connection to something which defies the natural order. (See the 'Echoes of Madness' adventure in Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt)  
- Dwarven cultists fanatically serve an unknown god, committing violence and atrocities at the behest of alien whispers in their minds. Where do these voices originate? (See "Waterfront District in Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt")  
- Sinister nightgaunts menace in the shadows, acting with unusual coordination. What is mustering them? (See [Nightgaunts](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/aberration/nightgaunt-sgtteh.md) in Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt)  

The common thread is using these minions to suggest something worse moving behind the scenes. Build a sense of escalation and reveal. First introduce the lesser threats, then slowly drop hints about the more unfathomable forces they serve—forbidden gods, alien intelligences, or cosmic forces.

### Total Descent into Cosmic Horror

When the time is right to unleash outright Lovecraftian horror, radically transform the world through apocalyptic events. This is something you'll want to do towards the end of your campaign, or at least the end of one of the arcs of your campaign, as it will be hard for your characters—and players—to come back from such insanity. This will best be explained by some examples:

#### The Stars Align

A rare celestial event causes the [Yellow Sign](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/decks/eldritch-moons-deck-sgtteh.md#Yellow%20Crown) to devour the sun. Gazing upon this impossible cosmic body drives people insane, sparking chaos and violence.

As the celestial event continues, the laws of physics become mutable. Unnatural weather, gravitational fluctuations, and color-draining light bathe the world. This heralds the imminent awakening of an eldritch horror beyond comprehension.

#### The Awakening God

In the depths of space, an ancient warring deity rejoins the pantheon of gods, awakening after eons of slumber. Its dream-projected nightmares transform forests into zones of madness. Across the world, cultists tremble in ecstasy as they prepare sacrifices for the Goat-Mother's awakening.

As the deity stirs, stars blot out and the planet develops lesions oozing forth alien fauna. The god's nascent thoughts alone unravel the fabric of reality as both mortal and divine minds quail before its cosmic power; the human empire was wiped out by her previous dream, and so will Luyarnha.

#### Opening Portals

Interdimensional portals split open the sky, revealing glimpses of strange nightmare realms beyond. Alien flora and fauna spill forth, defying natural law. Hordes of eldritch abominations flood through, driven by inscrutable hunger and malice.

Cults and osteomancers throughout the land further open these rifts, hoping to usher in their dark god and malformed idols. The barriers between dimensions erode rapidly, as the laws of physics degrade and warp.

By this stage, the world is beset by overt supernatural chaos. You can unleash these apocalypses as a failstate for your campaign if the heroes don't stop the villainous plot. Alien vistas, deranged cultists, and entities beyond comprehension—these elevate cosmic horror from unsettling clues to outright apocalyptic madness barely survivable by the remaining flicker of mortal minds.

## 4. Steinhardt's Mechanics

Here are some tips for integrating the madness and eldritch moon mechanics from *Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt* to enhance gothic and Lovecraftian horror in your 5e game:

### Madness

#### Using Madness Sparingly

When you first introduce madness mechanics, use them sparingly to preserve their dramatic impact. Reserve madness for significant story moments rather than routine failed ability checks or saving throws. Don't give indefinite madness for tripping in the streets—it's unfair and will cheapen the experience.

Introduce lesser versions of madness before inflicting indefinite madness. Short-term effects teach players the system before applying long-lasting impacts. Telegraph when a situation could induce more severe madness to maintain player agency.

#### Meaningful Madness Triggers

Inflict madness when it aligns with events in the story and makes thematic sense. Exposure to horrific rituals, forbidden knowledge, powerful aberrations, and harrowing experiences are fitting triggers. Madness can also arise from reading dark tomes, attuning to sinister artifacts, or communing with Great Old Ones. Make triggers relate to player actions rather than pure randomness.

#### Establish Madness Expectations

Provide players with clear guidance on actions that could induce madness. For example, witnessing a gruesome ritual may cause short-term madness, repeatedly reading from a profane tome can lead to long-term madness, while attuning to an eldritch artifact may inflict indefinite madness. Keep severity consistent.

#### Vivid Madness Descriptions

Thoroughly describe a character's descent into madness using evocative flavor text. Play up escalating physical and mental changes in unnerving detail. Here's an example:

> [!quote]  
> 
> Your throat burns painfully, reduced to a ragged rasp as you try to speak. You try again, but your voice deteriorates into a choked wheeze, then finally fades altogether. You find you cannot make a sound no matter how hard you try.
> 
> Your voice is gone, stolen by some unseen curse. Inside you might be screaming, but only isolating silence remains.
> 
> Trapped in a wordless solitude, it crosses your mind that this loss of voice may be merely the first step in a harrowing end. But what comes next, you cannot say... You are unraveling, strand by strand.

#### Impactful Madness Effects

The mechanical effects of madness alter capabilities beyond just penalties, such as imposing compulsions, modifying personality, removing proficiencies, or causing physical changes. This makes madness feel genuinely debilitating, so once a character gains the madness, make sure to create situations where the affliction feels crippling to capitalize on it.

This spotlight on the madness isn't malicious; it is destiny being indifferent to their plight (it just so happens that you, the GM, are destiny, but that's a minor detail). Once the gravity of the madness is understood, dial back the focus. The point is to create pressure, not to discourage players.

#### Curing Madness

Madness can be cured, but for it to be impactful, such treatment shouldn't come easily—or at all. Follow the rules outlined in Steinhardt's Guide, but remember that not all is curable.

#### Tracking Madness Occurences

Carefully track how often and how frequently madness affects each character. Gaining multiple shortterm madnesses within a brief period can lead to worse forms. So keep a counter for the number of each type of madness the PCs gain.

### Eldritch Moons

Here's a short guide to seamlessly integrate the moons of Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt into your 5e world building and gameplay:

#### Foreshadowing the Cycles

When you introduce the moons, don't overwhelm players all at once. Provide subtle hints initially and slowly reveal more information as the game progresses. Mention offhand comments by NPCs about dreading the approach of a certain moon or alluding to past events triggered by a lunar cycle. Let players discover moon lore books detailing their effects. Start with rumor and superstition, then gradually confirm which parts are true. Luyarnha is plagued with ignorances, and the characters shouldn't always have the correct information right away.

#### Scaling the Impact

As your players become more familiar with the moons, increase their impact and frequency. Keep usage minimal at lower levels when characters are still vulnerable. You can use the "Eldritch Beckoning method" to ensure that the progression is smooth and that player actions impact the world around them. Let them figure out on their own that the osteomancy they've been using corrupts the fabric of the world.

As they gain experience and abilities, they will be better equipped to handle moon-related challenges. Eventually, moons can be an anticipated occurrence that must be prepared for.

#### Raising Tension and Threats

Use moons to introduce new threats and challenges tied to their themes. The [glacial moon](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/decks/eldritch-moons-deck-sgtteh.md#Glacial%20Moon) could awaken a frozen god threatening the land with an eternal winter (e.g., the Deicide adventure of Steinhardt's Guide). The blood moon can empower a corrupt lord, enabling it to finally finish transforming into an ancient dread form. Implement side quests to discover methods of counteracting or protecting against moon effects.

#### Worldbuilding Integration

Incorporate moons into your world's lore and history notes. In Steinhardt's Guide, past events can be attributed to certain moons, with the most notable among them being the Hunts tied to the Blood Moon of Rebirth. Prophecies and myths can foretell what will occur when a moon cycle converges with a cosmic alignment or milestone date. Moon effects can influence architecture, festivals, crops, magic, and more. NPC behaviors will shift based on the present moon. The lore of Steinhardt's Guide is fluid in that regard; you can either stick to it entirely or alter details to make your own devilish concoction.

#### Encouraging Strategy

Eventually, you should share moon information with the players so they can strategize around cycles. Preparedness, vigilance, and modified tactics may be key to surviving certain lunar events. Offer options to mitigate disadvantages, like lanterns that shield an area from the [Vacuous Moon's](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/decks/eldritch-moons-deck-sgtteh.md#Vacuous%20Moon) darkness. Planning journeys around cycles or staying indoors can be crucial. Knowledgeable NPCs can provide warnings, advice, and moon-related services.

By gradually establishing moons as an integral part of your world through lore, descriptions, and gameplay impact, they will feel like a seamless and immersive mechanic enhancing your gothic or Lovecraftian horror campaign. Players will be invested in moon cycles, eagerly preparing for their favorites while dreading those that endanger their survival.

#### Getting Players to Brave the Moonlight

While eldritch moons inject exciting challenges and variation into your TTRPG campaign, some players may try to avoid this by keeping their characters sheltered indoors at night. As a GM, you can discourage this in various ways.

- **Plot Progression Outdoors.** Schedule key story moments, battles, rituals, and other events outdoors at night, compelling important progression under the moonlight.  
- **Escalating Threats.** Moon-empowered monsters and villains grow progressively stronger and bolder the longer they stay under the moon, eventually forcing confrontation.  
- **Incentives for Nighttime Exploration.** Provide rewards like rare loot, treasures, and valuable knowledge that can only be gained by braving the moonlit darkness. High risk brings higher rewards.  
- **Empowering Moons.** Balance harmful moons with equipment that only provides boons and buffs to characters while under the moonlight, making nighttime activity slightly less dangerous.  
- **Light Source Limitations.** During certain moons, light sources are unreliable except for the revealing moonlight itself. The darkness becomes its own threat.  
- **Inescapable Dangers.** Have some moon effects, such as maddening auras, environmental transformations, or insane NPC behavior, still impact characters even while indoors. Total safety should not be guaranteed.  
- **Social Pressure.** Have NPCs mock, shame, or express disappointment in characters hiding from moons rather than confronting the challenges heroically.  
- **Consequences of Inaction.** If players hide from the moons, let villainous plots advance unopposed, showing the costly consequences. Guilt can motivate.  
- **Revealing Moonlight.** The lunar light exposes hidden locations, mystical runes, or secret passages that characters will want to discover.  

By making moonlit adventures tempting, mechanically meaningful, and at times unavoidable, you can ensure your players fully engage with this fun added dimension of your *Steinhardt's Guide* game.
