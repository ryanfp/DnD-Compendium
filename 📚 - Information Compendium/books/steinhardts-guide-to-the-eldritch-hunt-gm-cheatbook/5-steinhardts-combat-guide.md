# Steinhardt's Combat Guide
*Source: Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt: GM Cheatbook, p. 56* 

In this section, I'll share rules and tips that speed up combat, build tension, and bring horror elements into your 5e combats. You'll also learn how to handle large horde fights without getting overwhelmed. With these guidelines, you can orchestrate intense battles your players will love. Combat is a chance to build drama and suspense, not just a time-filler.

I'll also cover setting scenes, making resource management matter, using ambushes and traps, and introducing moral dilemmas. For each of these, I'll include examples showing how to apply the advice.

## Making Combat Faster

*Gas gas gas, we need to step on the gas!*

Why should combat be faster in 5e? When players have to wait too long for their turn, it can become boring and break the game's flow. If combat is quicker, everyone stays involved, and the game feels more energetic and exciting.

Below are various tips I use to make combat faster. I use all of them, but I find that the first two are the ones that will save you the most time.

### Use Static Monster Damage

Not having to roll and calculate damage on every monster attack saves an incredible amount of time during combat and makes no difference to the flow of the game. Multiple attacks, different attacks from certain monsters, and attacks from multiple monsters will provide more than enough variation to the amount of damage characters are taking.

Most 5th edition monsters, except the really old ones, are provided with the average of the monster's damage roll. Some think that this makes the monsters too predictable, but with the average round of combat lasting 3 rounds and most monsters in the book having multiple attack types, your players won't have time to figure out you're using the average damage before the monsters—or they—die. If you're really afraid of predictability, choose a number from 1 through 4 and add (or subtract) that number to the damage as you see fit. This will save SO much time.

To put my money where my mouth is, I ran an example combat twice with two different parties. Each fight was against 6 creatures—2 [corvian dwellers](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/miscellaneous/corvian-dweller-sgtteh.md) and 4 [scourge lycans](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/miscellaneous/scourge-lycan-sgtteh.md)—and I recorded the time it took for me to complete my turn as a GM in each. In the first fight with each party I rolled for damage, and in the second fight with each I took the average.

**I saved 107% time per turn on average** by taking the average compared to rolling and calculating damage. **That's over twice as fast!** If that doesn't convince you to use average, I don't know what will.

The bigger the scale of the combat the more I recommend doing this; if you have to calculate damage for every single mob in the horde, it will take forever.

> [!note] A note on spells:
> 
> The only time where the average damage isn't shown is on spellcasters. For these creatures, calculate the average yourself and write them in your notes before your sessions. You can determine the average damage quickly by multiplying the number of dice by the average for each die. The average for each die is shown below (always round down):
> 
> |    |    |    |    |    |    |
> |----|----|----|----|----|----|
> | `D4`: 2.5 | `D6`: 3.5 | `D8`: 4.5 | `D10`: 5.5 | `D12`: 6.5 | `D20`: 10.5 |

### Delegate Initiative Tracking and Other Tasks

You have many things to handle during combat, especially in encounters featuring large numbers of monsters. At the same time, the players are typically focused only on their single characters, with a fair bit of downtime in between each character's turn. As such, delegating some of your tasks to the players during combat can make your life as a GM significantly easier and keep the players more engaged in the game.

Handling initiative is one of my go-tos. At the start of the session, or the combat, ask one of the players to take on the job of writing down initiative rolls and keeping track of the order of combat. Then, when battle begins, initiative becomes one less thing you need to worry about.

If the players enjoy taking on additional tasks, you might consider delegating other combat-focused activities, such as tracking monster damage.

You'd be surprised how much time you can save by letting your players help you during the combat, and it is really fun for players when they see the amount of damage they can rack up on your enemies. The only exception I make to that rule is for enemies capable of regenerating health; keeping players in the dark in regards to how much a monster has healed adds an element of uncertainty.

### Reveal the Monster's AC

Once the characters have attacked the monster a few times, reveal the AC of the creature so players can figure out if they hit or miss without having to consult you. You can even write it down and show it to them so they can reference it during the fight.

### Rulings

In 5e, rule disagreements can rise up, and they can bog down combat if everyone pulls out their phone (or adds their two cents) to check which interpretation of the rules is correct. It kills the flow and slows down combat, two things which we do not want to happen.

So what do you do in those situations?

I use the following guideline: the GM makes the final call, so for the duration of the combat, we will use their ruling. At the same time, any players in disagreement can write down what they think is wrong on a piece of paper or make a mental note.

Then, at the end of the session, you decide as a group what the correct ruling is and will know for future sessions.

> [!note] Note on the Rule of Cool:
> 
> Most of the time it's better to follow the written rules, but as the GM, it's your game. Feel free to ignore a ruling you think doesn't fit or isn't fun. Use what fits your table best.
> 
> Sometimes as a GM you may choose to apply the rule of cool and let the players do something awesome that the rules would otherwise not allow. If that's the case, just let your players know, hey this is 'rule of cool' and how I'm ruling for this particular moment in time. It might not affect future events, but for now, this is what is happening.
> 
> I like to specify that it doesn't affect future rulings, because later down the line you may realize that what you allowed is game-breaking, and you might want to readjust.
^note-on-the-rule-of-cool

### Run Theater of the Mind

Battle maps can be fun for big crunchy battles with lots of different monsters and interesting terrain. For quick skirmishes, you can run combat in the theater of the mind (a.k.a. your imagination) or use a quick abstract battle map. Setting up PCs on a battle map and deciding how to move them around can drag on. So for these occasions, theater of the mind can save valuable time. It works best when you don't have a map ready or when you're too lazy to prepare one such map.

Most battles don't need to be big knock down, drag out slugfests. Keep theater of the mind combat in your toolbox and use it for battles where positioning isn't nearly as important.

### Have Alternative Outs

In a given fight, you should have alternate means for the combat to end beyond the default of "one side is dead", which doesn't leave much room for nuance and can take unnecessary time.

To start, take a moment to put yourself in the situation and in the mind of the enemies your party is fighting. Now make sure you know their goals:

*Why are they fighting? What do they want to get out of it? Would they be open to negotiation? Is their heart really in the fight, or is something else forcing the conflict? Are any of the bad guys in the fight in charge?*

From there, you can develop alternatives to letting the fight continue to the bitter end. Some examples that could result from the above questions:

- The Forsaken want an item from the party and will focus on the character with the item. If they can steal it, they'll run away from the fight with it.  
- The enemies are a mercenary company. If too many of them are bloodied or killed, they'll stage a tactical retreat. Alternatively, they respond well to bribes.  
- The Unseen Claw operate as a brave unified fighting force—until there's only one of them left. Then he or she pleads for their life.  
- A crazed osteomancer has taken control of unwilling creatures to help him fight. They are bound to his magic, so if he is killed, they will be freed.  
- The Goat-Mother, fueled by the power of her nightmare realm, is destroying reality itself around the PCs. They can't fight her, they can only hope to escape through the portal... which is guarded by her spawns.  
- Malach draws his power from multiple generators set up in his lair. By destroying them, much of his power is cut off.  
- The flight of screechwings is only interested in hit and run tactics. They will not stand and fight, but instead engage, deal some damage, and then fly away.  
- The manikins are all armed with self-destructing batteries, in case their master is killed.  

As you can see, not only do you have a technique to shorten combats, you also have an effective technique to build interesting encounters. Use of this method encourages skill checks and challenges, dialogue, role playing, and creativity in combat.

Finally, if it becomes obvious that the party will absolutely crush the rest of the encounter, offer to end the encounter early. They'll take the damage from a round of combat randomly distributed, and the fight will end.

## Weaving Horror Inside of 5e Combat

While 5e is a versatile system capable of handling a variety of themes and tones, it's not inherently designed for horror. The empowerment fantasy at the core of most 5e campaigns can sometimes clash with the disempowerment typically associated with horror. However, there are ways to adjust your approach to combat to better serve a horror-themed campaign. Here's how:

### Rebalancing Power Dynamics

#### Limit Resources


One of the quickest ways to instill fear is to make resources scarce. Whether it's ammunition, healing potions, or spell slots, scarcity can make every decision in combat feel weighty and terrifying. See the Rest Changes for Dark Fantasy section above for detailed advice to do just that.

#### Increase Vulnerability


Consider mechanics that weaken the players. This could be environmental factors, like darkness or fog, or conditions imposed by enemies, like curses, poisons or madness.

As a rule of thumb, avoid using effects and abilities that remove player agency. Effects that remove the player from the game, like being stunned, paralyzed, or banished, aren't fun. Instead, make them struggle with other effects, such as frequent disadvantage, harder checks, increase the damage dealt to them, or lower their damage output via resistances and other means.

### Unpredicatable Combat

In a horror-themed 5e campaign, combat encounters should be more than just physical confrontations; they should also serve as psychological battlegrounds. Let your players have their regular all-out brawls, but don't be afraid to mix and match some psychological elements in there too. Here are some of most common ways you can unsettle your players in combat:

- **Ambushes and Traps.** Use ambushes and traps to keep players on their toes. The sudden onset of danger can serve as a psychological jolt, increasing tension.  
- **Unpredictable Enemies.** Employ creatures that can change form, become invisible, or use illusions. The uncertainty of what the enemy will do next adds a psychological layer to the combat.  
- **Madness.** Introduce madness saves during combat when players witness something truly horrifying. A failed check could impose disadvantages or—infrequently—temporary loss of control over actions (activating friendly fire for example).  
- **Fear.** Use spells or monster abilities that induce fear. If a character is frightened, they become less efficient in combat; if the entire party becomes frightened, fleeing becomes a tempting option.  
- **Non-Combatants.** Introduce innocent bystanders or hostages into the combat scenario. Players will have to decide whether to focus solely on the enemy or try to save others, adding a moral dilemma to the fight.  
- **Friendly Fire.** Use scenarios where the enemy is indistinguishable from allies or uses them as shields. This forces players to think twice before attacking.  
- **High Stakes.** Make the consequences of using powerful spells or items clear. For example, using a powerful artifact might draw the attention of an even more terrifying cosmic entity. Check out "page 283 of Steinhardt's Guide to the Eldritch Hunt" for more detailed information about this.  
- **Taunts and Distractions.** Have enemies use psychological tactics, like taunting or revealing unsettling truths, to throw characters off balance.  
- **False Information.** Use illusions or deceptive tactics to make players question the reality of the combat situation. Is that really the enemy, or is it an illusion?

#### Running the Encounter

##### Thrown Corpse:

If the players stay in the street, seeing the child's maimed corpse falling from the roof is enough to scar their mind. Force a Wisdom saving throw against madness on the whole party.

Make it clear that failure has high stakes. If a lycan lands a bite, describe the rotten substance that drips from their jaw, force a madness save, and describe how the affected player sees a vision of them turning into a deformed monster, adding another layer of dread.

Throughout the combat, use descriptive language to heighten the horror. Describe the lycans' snarls, the foul glint of their fangs in the torchlight, and the inhumane force of their attacks.

## Horde Combat

Or how to throw 100 enemies at your players and make it fun.

### I. Damage Tracking for the Horde

- Note down the number of monsters within the horde  
- Note down the hit points of one typical monster within the horde (you can round to the nearest 5 or 10 for simpler calculations).  
- Consolidate all damage received by the horde into a single hit point pool.  
- When accumulated damage exceeds the hit points of one or multiple monsters, eliminate monsters from the horde and reset the damage count. Any excess damage should be carried over.  

#### Example

The party is engaging a horde of 20 skeletons. A skeleton has an average of 13 hit points, but we will round it down to 10 to make things simple. There are 20 skeletons in your horde.

Your notes should look like this:

|    |    |
|----|----|
| *Number of creatures (Nb): 20* | *HP (out of 10): 10* |

The barbarian rushes in and lands a big critical hit for a total of 27 points of damage. With that damage, 2 skeletons go down, and the damage carries over. So now your notes should look like this:

|    |    |
|----|----|
| *Number of creatures (Nb): ~~20~~ 18* | *HP (out of 10): ~~10~~ 3* |

And you keep going until the number of skeletons drops to 0 (or the number of party members does).

### II. Horde Attacks and Damage Distribution

Assign which horde monsters attack which character. Start by looking at the situation. Does the horde have ranged attacks? Are any of the characters up front and likely to take more hits than everyone else?

**Our base assumption should be that the monsters attack the characters equally.**

If there are 50 skeletons and 5 characters, 10 skeletons will attack each character. If one character manages to hide from the skeletons, the skeletons divide their attacks among the rest.

If a scenario evolves and the monsters can't attack, they deal no damage. For example, if there is a choke point, and the skeletons don't have ranged attacks, they can't hit the players.

> [!note] Note:
> 
> An isolated character that is completely surrounded can be targeted by a maximum number of 8 creatures in melee.
^note

To save time in combat, if you are preparing to make your players take on a horde, use average damage to not spend hours rolling damage dice (see "page 57").

To determine if an attack hits for each creature, roll a `d4` instead of a `d20`. Use the following attack success rate guidelines:

- **Base.** A roll of 1–2 is a miss, and a roll of 3–4 is a hit.  
- **With Advantage.** Only a 1 misses.  
- **With Disadvantage.** Only a 4 hits.  

If a creature has a very high AC, or gains a higher AC through casting the shield spell—something that only a 15+ on the `d20` used for their normal attack roll would hit—consider making only a 4 hit. Consider asking your players for their AC before combat so you can adjust the attack rolls accordingly.

#### Example

If we take our skeletons as above, they have a `+4` to hit. This means that any AC above 19 is considered very high AC for this fight, and anything below is considered normal AC.

So our barbarian who charged into the fray is now surrounded by 8 skeletons. He only has 17 AC, which means we use the base rolls. We roll 8 d4s (`8d4`). 3 of them show a 3 or 4, so that's 3 hits. Using the average damage, our barbarian takes 15 damage.

8 attacks and their damage is wrapped up in less than a minute—very nice!

### III. Handling Area of Effect (AoE) Attacks

#### _Determine Affected Monsters_


Assess the area impacted and judge the number of creatures within. Favor densely packed scenarios for dramatic effects.

Rough estimates for creatures affected by AoE size:

- Small AoE (e.g., flaming sphere): 4 creatures  
- Medium AoE (e.g., burning hands): 8 creatures  
- Large AoE (e.g., fireball): 16 creatures  
- Huge AoE (e.g., circle of death): 32 creatures  

#### _Resolve Saving Throws and Damage:_

Horde monsters are usually weaker grunts. Typically, only 1 in 4 monsters succeed on saving throws. Adjust this based on context; if you use monsters with very high Dexterity scores, consider making 1 in 2 succeed against Dexterity saves instead.

In exceptional or heroic moments, consider having all monsters fail their saves.

If the AoE damage surpasses the hit points of an individual monster, remove those affected. Any residual damage after exact kills should be added to the horde's damage tally, which might result in further eliminations.

#### _Manage Status Effects:_

If an AoE confers a status effect, assume 1 in 4 of the affected creatures succeed and are afflicted. These creatures may be narratively described or temporarily removed from combat to avoid meticulous tracking.

#### Example

It's now the wizard's turn, and she casts the shatter spell. Considered a medium AoE, it catches 8 creatures.

She rolls `3d8` for damage: 14 thunder damage!

Our horde skeletons have 10 hit points. Using the AoE rules above, 1 in 4 succeed. So, for 8 creatures, 2 succeed on the saving throw and 6 fail. These 6 skeletons instantly die.

So our note for the hordes would look like this:

|    |    |
|----|----|
| Nb: 20 | HP (out of 10): 10 |
| Nb: ~~20~~ 14 | HP (out of 10): 10 |

The remaining 2 in the AoE take 7 damage each, but we count the horde as one entity, so our notes would then look like this:

|    |    |
|----|----|
| Nb: ~~20~~ 14 | HP (out of 10): 10 |
|  | Damage taken: 7 + 7 = 14 |
| Nb: ~~20~~ ~~14~~ 13 | HP (out of 10): ~~10~~ 6 |

So her spell killed 7 skeletons and damaged an 8th one.

I broke down the math to be as clear as possible, but there's no need to write everything out in your own notes.

### IV. Additional Horde Management Tips

#### Narrative Focus:

Emphasize the story. Instead of detailing mechanics, paint a vivid picture of hordes charging, spells detonating, and heroes standing resilient. This is an epic moment; don't get bogged down by mechanics too much.

#### Reflavoring Creatures:

Use one stat block for different monsters in the horde. Describe variations in appearance or behavior without altering mechanics. You can say that the horde is composed of zombies and skeletons, but only use one of these stat blocks for your entire horde.

#### Combine Horde with Single Entities:

Using a horde alongside distinct creatures (e.g., a zombie horde with a [scourge lycan](/📚%20-%20Information%20Compendium/bestiary/miscellaneous/scourge-lycan-sgtteh.md) that gets its own actual stat block) creates a more layered combat scenario.
